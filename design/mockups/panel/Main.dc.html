How an amoeba moves

Freshwater protists, and the mechanics of the pseudopod

An amoeba moves by extending a pseudopod, a temporary projection of the cell body, and then flowing the rest of itself into the space the projection has made. Nothing about the shape is permanent.

The mechanism is a change of state in the cytoplasm. The outer layer stiffens into a gel while the interior stays fluid, and pressure from the stiffened region drives the fluid forward. Reverse the two and the cell retracts instead.

Feeding uses the same motion. Pseudopods surround a food particle until they meet, enclosing it in a vacuole that is then digested inside the cell. The process is called phagocytosis, and it is how most free-living amoebae eat.

Because there is no fixed front or back, direction is set moment to moment by where the cell happens to be stiffening.
